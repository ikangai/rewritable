Design lookbook · v2 · advisory-only editing

The intelligence chip, four ways.

A droppable AI is an awkward object to design: it is structured like a form (a prompt, an engine, credentials, a signature), it must be editable like any rewritable, and it must be browsable in a gallery. These four directions each take a wildly different stance on what a droppable intelligence even is — a card to collect, a datasheet to read, a person to meet, a machine to operate. Same real content in every one. v2: the edit demos now honour the editing model you chose — advisory-only: the signed personality is frozen, and only the engine/affinity change by prompt.

Edit it with a prompt

Why it works

A binder of foil cards is genuinely fun to browse, and the foil-vs-matte split turns the signature — normally a boring shield — into the single most eye-catching thing on the card. Capability reads as "stats", which is honest here because they map to real fields.

The risk

Gamified stats can read as unserious for a tool people trust with their writing. Mitigation: the stats are real (engine/affinity/reach), never invented numbers, and the copy stays plain.

02

The Datasheet

An AI is a spec you read top to bottom — a component datasheet / punch-card manifest. Structure is the aesthetic.
metaphor engineering datasheet editing command line · signed writes rejected trust checksum stamp vibe brutalist blueprint, mono

Committed cool-blueprint world, and the natural home for advisory-only editing: ink fields are signed & frozen, cyan fields are advisory & editable — the command line simply refuses to write the ink. Every field is labelled, aligned, monospaced; you edit by typing.

Why it works

It never pretends to be something it isn't. Signed vs advisory fields are visually distinct (ink vs cyan), the checksum stamp makes trust unambiguous, and "editing = typing a command" is the truest expression of a rewritable. It reads as trustworthy infrastructure.

The risk

Cold. A gallery of datasheets may not seduce a first-time visitor the way a portrait or a foil card does. Bet: precision and the red REJECT stamp carry enough drama for the audience that actually cares.

Committed cool-ivory world with a deep-teal voice and a brass credential seal — deliberately not the cream-and-terracotta editorial cliché. The system prompt becomes a pull-quote: the one line that lets you hear the voice before you hire it.

Edit it with a prompt

Why it works

It's the warmest, most browsable direction — a roster of characters invites the "who do I want in the room?" feeling. "Brief it" reframes prompt-editing as delegation, which is exactly the mental model of handing a document to an editor.

The risk · B costs it the most

Its signature move was reshaping the voice by briefing — exactly what advisory-only editing forbids. Now the persona can only decline in-character and switch engines, so the "hire an editor you can direct" promise shrinks to "hire a fixed editor, swap its engine." Plus: anthropomorphising is opinionated (monogram avatars stay abstract, not a fake photo), and the persona-graduation path is deferred, so don't over-promise personhood.

04

The Instrument

An AI is a piece of gear you rack, patch, and tune. The engine and routing are knobs you turn; the voice is a sealed, signed module.
metaphor synth module / rack unit editing patch the engine · voice params locked trust engraved serial + lit LED vibe graphite, industrial, LEDs

Committed graphite-anodized world. Connection state — the one thing that's session-only and easy to lose — becomes a hardware power LED: green when connected, amber when a model is recommended but not wired, dark when off. Semantic colour, kept separate from the accent.

Edit it with a prompt

Why it works

It solves the real UX problem the others gloss over: connection state is invisible and easy to lose. As a power LED it's the first thing you see. "Tune a parameter" makes prompt-editing feel precise and reversible rather than a black box.

The risk · B locks its best knob

The expressive move — the warmth fader — is a signed voice parameter, so under advisory-only editing it's frozen; only the engine/routing knobs turn. A tactile surface that promises "tune everything" but greys out its most tempting control invites the exact frustration B is meant to avoid. (Also: skeuomorphism dates fast — kept matte, no glossy bevels — so it leans hardest on execution.)

Where this stands

Two decisions are locked in. Editing model = advisory-only (B): a chip's personality is a signed, frozen artifact; only its engine/affinity change by prompt, and "make it different" routes to the Maker. Surface = both the cold gallery listing and the in-app chip — editing governs only the in-app side, so the gallery cards stay pure listings. Every edit demo above now reflects that.

Pick a lead B favours 02 Datasheet and 01 Card — their "edit = configure" story survives intact. It undercuts 03 & 04, whose charm was reshaping the voice.
Steal parts e.g. the Datasheet's signed/advisory colour-coding + the Instrument's connection LED. Mixes welcome.
Kill list Anything that's an obvious no, so I stop spending effort there.
Still open · skills A real per-persona skills array, or is model + affinity + vault enough?
